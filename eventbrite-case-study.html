What the job actually was

Eventbrite publishes the same guides for several markets. The US blog gets the new content first; the UK blog lagged behind, with older versions, US examples, dollar prices and links into the .com site. AS Marketing was contracted to close that gap: 25 US articles a month, rewritten for UK readers and republished on eventbrite.co.uk.

Six people touched every article: a writer produced the UK copy from a content brief, a second reviewer checked it, an image editor localised screenshots and graphics, I built the page in WordPress, the project lead ran the agency QA, and Eventbrite's content team did the final QA and published. The shared workflow sheet had one row per article and one column per hand-off, so at any moment everyone could see where each of the 25 pieces was stuck.

Output and revision rounds

The revision notes in March were the useful part. They were specific and repetitive: remove the first of two CTAs, add an image before each main heading, lower-case CTA captions, no full stop in the caption, replace US screenshots. I turned that list into a pre-upload checklist and applied it to every article before it reached the client. That is the whole story behind the drop from 25 to 0.

SEO checks per article

Rank Math is a plugin score, not a ranking. I used it as a consistency gate: if 25 articles a month go live, a cheap automated check that every one of them has the focus keyword in title, URL, meta description and first paragraph is worth more than a careful manual review of three of them.

What I learned

Finding 1 — Consistency beats brilliance at 25 articles a month.

Nothing about a single article was difficult. Getting 176 of them through six hands without losing one was the job. The shared sheet with one column per hand-off did more for quality than any individual edit.

Finding 2 — Client revision notes are a free specification.

The first month's 25 sets of notes contained maybe eight distinct rules. Written down once and applied before upload, they removed the revision round almost entirely.

Finding 3 — Localisation is mostly links and examples, not words.

Spelling was the easy part. The real UK work was replacing US event examples, dollar prices, US-only tools (Keela → NeonCRM), US downloadables with no UK equivalent, and every internal link that pointed into eventbrite.com.

Finding 4 — Know what you cannot claim.

Eventbrite's own team owned the SEO report and the rankings. I never had access to the UK blog's Search Console, so this case study shows process and quality metrics, not traffic. Where I do have the traffic data, I show it: see the Tajaddawa case study.
