Project Overview

Rockbuster Stealth, an online video rental service, was preparing to launch a streaming platform and needed an evidence-based view of revenue before allocating the launch budget. The leadership team posed four questions: which films contribute the most and least to revenue, who pays the highest lifetime totals, what does the customer geographic footprint look like, and is that footprint concentrated in a few cities or spread thinly across many?

The full analysis was carried out in PostgreSQL against the company's 15-table snowflake schema (3 fact, 2 bridge, 10 dimension tables — see the ERD and data dictionary) and surfaced through a four-tab Tableau Public dashboard for executive consumption.

Catalog Snapshot

Before slicing the customer base, I built a numerical descriptive profile of the film catalog and ran data-quality checks. None of the columns in film or customer contained NULLs.

Read — A flexible, mid-priced catalog

The catalog spans short and full-length films across the full $0.99–$4.99 pricing band. Pricing is essentially uniform on average, so revenue differences come from who is renting and how often, not from variation in ticket size.

Geographic Distribution

The first SQL technique that actually drives a strategic answer is a four-table join walking from customer through address → city → country:

SELECT
  co.country,
  COUNT(DISTINCT cu.customer_id) AS customer_count
FROM customer AS cu
JOIN address AS a  ON cu.address_id = a.address_id
JOIN city    AS ci ON a.city_id     = ci.city_id
JOIN country AS co ON ci.country_id = co.country_id
GROUP BY co.country
ORDER BY customer_count DESC
LIMIT 10;

Key Insight — Choose the right granularity

The same JOIN that produces a clean ranked list of countries collapses into a flat distribution of single-customer cities. Country is the analytically meaningful unit for go-to-market planning; city is too sparse to support marketing decisions.

Customer Revenue Concentration

The next question — the average spend of the top 5 highest-paying customers — is where the analysis moves from joins into nested logic. Reusing the country-level query as an inline subquery and wrapping it in AVG():

SELECT AVG(top_5.total_amount_paid) AS average
FROM (
  SELECT
    cu.customer_id, cu.first_name, cu.last_name,
    co.country, ci.city,
    SUM(p.amount) AS total_amount_paid
  FROM customer AS cu
  JOIN address AS a  ON cu.address_id = a.address_id
  JOIN city    AS ci ON a.city_id     = ci.city_id
  JOIN country AS co ON ci.country_id = co.country_id
  JOIN payment AS p  ON cu.customer_id = p.customer_id
  WHERE ci.city IN (
    'Aurora','Acua','Adana','Adoni','Ahmadnagar',
    'Akishima','Akron','Allapuzha (Alleppey)','Allende','Alvorada'
  )
  GROUP BY cu.customer_id, cu.first_name, cu.last_name, co.country, ci.city
  ORDER BY total_amount_paid DESC
  LIMIT 5
) AS top_5;

Result: $108.54 average. The same logic was also rewritten as a CTE for readability — same answer, cleaner structure.

Key Insight — Retention beats acquisition for the top tier

The top 5 customers cluster tightly in the $98–$135 range and average $108.54, well above the typical ticket. Concierge service, early access and personalised recommendations for this segment are cheaper than acquiring equivalent revenue from new customers.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — Customer volume concentrates in five countries.

India (60), China (53), the United States (36), Japan (31), and Mexico (30) anchor the international footprint. These five markets should drive the bulk of regional marketing spend.

Finding 2 — City-level targeting is not actionable.

Aurora is the only city with more than one customer; every other city in the top 10 has exactly one. City-level segmentation produces too much noise to drive strategy — country is the right unit.

Finding 3 — A small group of repeat renters drives outsized revenue.

The top 5 customers each spend $98–$135, averaging $108.54. A premium retention programme aimed at high-value renters is more cost-effective than equivalent acquisition.

Finding 4 — Pricing variation is not a revenue lever.

Rental rates span $0.99–$4.99 with an average of $2.98 and the catalog skews PG-13. Differences in revenue come from customer behaviour and geography, not from ticket size — so growth comes from who rents, not what they pay per rental.
