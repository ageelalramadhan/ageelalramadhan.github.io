Project Overview

The 2019 World Happiness Report ranks 156 countries on a self-reported happiness score and pairs each with six socio-economic variables — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. The analysis combines correlation, univariate regression, and K-Means clustering to ask which combinations of factors actually segregate happier from unhappier countries.

A separate time-series demonstration on U.S. unemployment data (FRED UNRATE, monthly) is included as a methodological exhibit — happiness is cross-sectional (one snapshot per country), so time-series tools like ADF stationarity tests and autocorrelation diagnostics need a different dataset to demonstrate.

Hypotheses: H1 — Higher GDP per capita correlates with higher happiness. H2 — Social support is positively associated with happiness. H3 — Healthy life expectancy is positively associated with happiness.

Correlation Structure

The correlation matrix is the clearest summary of the dataset. GDP per capita, social support, and healthy life expectancy each correlate with happiness in the 0.77–0.79 range — close enough that no single one of them dominates the others. Freedom adds a moderate signal (r ≈ 0.57); corruption perceptions a weaker one (r ≈ 0.39); generosity is essentially uncorrelated (r ≈ 0.08).

Key Insight — GDP, social support, life expectancy all carry comparable weight

All three top predictors correlate with happiness at almost identical strength (≈0.78). They're also tightly correlated with each other (≈0.72–0.84), so they share much of their explanatory signal. The univariate regressions in this analysis cannot disentangle which dominates — that would require multivariate regression and is outside this project's scope.

GDP per Capita and Happiness

The bivariate relationship is strongly positive: wealthier countries report higher happiness scores. The Pearson coefficient is r = 0.794, the highest of the six predictors. But the scatter shows substantial within-band variance — countries at similar GDP levels still produce noticeably different happiness scores, which is what motivates examining the other factors.

Social Support and Happiness

Social support — the share of respondents who say they have someone to count on in times of trouble — correlates with happiness at r = 0.777, statistically indistinguishable from GDP at this sample size. Its scatter has a similar shape: a clear positive trend with substantial residual variance.

Key Insight — Social support is co-equal, not dominant

An earlier framing of this analysis claimed social support was a "stronger and more consistent" predictor than GDP. The data does not support that conclusion. The two correlations (0.794 vs. 0.777) are too close to declare a winner without multivariate analysis, and the project did not perform one. The honest reading: both matter, comparably.

Global Happiness Map

Mapping the 2019 happiness scores to country boundaries makes the geographic pattern visible at a glance. Northern Europe and North America score highest; sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia score lowest. Within most regions there is still substantial spread.

K-Means Clustering — Well-Being Profiles

To move beyond pairwise relationships, the four strongest predictors (GDP, social support, life expectancy, freedom) were standardised and passed to K-Means with k = 3. The resulting clusters segment countries cleanly along the GDP-and-happiness diagonal:

Key Insight — Three coherent profiles emerge from the multivariate space

Multivariate clustering produces three roughly equal-sized groups that map cleanly onto colloquial categories of "lower", "medium", and "higher" national well-being. The clusters carry information that no single pairwise correlation captures — they reflect the joint structure of the four standardised dimensions, not just one of them.

Time-Series Methodology Exhibit

The happiness dataset is cross-sectional — one row per country, one year. To demonstrate time-series technique, the modelling notebook includes a separate exhibit on U.S. monthly unemployment (FRED UNRATE, 1990–present). This is intentionally not integrated with the happiness analysis; it is included to show the difference in approach when the data is temporally ordered.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — GDP per capita is the single strongest correlate of happiness (r = 0.794).

Wealthier countries report higher average wellbeing — the basic income–happiness link is confirmed, with GDP alone explaining roughly 63 % of cross-country variance.

Finding 2 — Social support and life expectancy are co-equal with GDP, not stronger.

Pearson correlations: GDP 0.794, life expectancy 0.780, social support 0.777. The three are statistically indistinguishable at n = 156 and are themselves highly intercorrelated (r ≈ 0.72–0.84). Untangling them would need multivariate regression.

Finding 3 — Generosity is essentially uncorrelated with national happiness (r ≈ 0.08).

Within the WHR-2019 framing, generosity scores carry almost no cross-country signal for the happiness score, even though they correlate moderately with freedom and corruption perceptions.

Finding 4 — K-Means produces three coherent well-being profiles.

Standardising the four strongest predictors and clustering at k = 3 yields Lower (41), Medium (56), and Higher (59) well-being groups that align with the GDP × happiness diagonal and carry multivariate information beyond any single correlation.

Limitations

  • Single-year snapshot — no temporal trend can be inferred for happiness itself.
  • Correlations do not imply causation; the analysis identifies association strength, not causal direction.
  • The regressions in this project are univariate; relative contributions of correlated predictors cannot be separated without a multivariate model.
  • K-Means with k = 3 is exploratory — alternative k values and clustering methods would refine the segmentation.
