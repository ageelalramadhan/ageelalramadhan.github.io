Project Overview

Analysis of U.S. influenza mortality across all 50 states from 2009 through 2017. The core insight: mortality rate, not raw death counts, is the correct metric for cross-state comparison — a methodological choice that fundamentally changes what the data communicates.

Business question: Are some states more dangerous than others for influenza, and is mortality changing over time?

Temporal Trend (2009–2017)

Annual deaths remained broadly stable across the study period. Statistical testing of year-over-year mortality rates shows no significant change — the rate has hovered consistently around 0.012% nationally.

Key Insight — No significant change over time

Year-over-year statistical tests confirm mortality rate has remained essentially flat across 2009–2017. Apparent volume increases reflect population growth, not worsening outcomes.

Spatial Distribution by State

Mapping raw death counts produces a misleading picture: California, Texas, and New York appear most dangerous simply because they are the most populous. Switching the metric to mortality rate per 100,000 reveals a fundamentally different geography of risk.

Key Insight — Choose the metric, change the answer

Choosing mortality rate over raw counts fundamentally changes what the data says — a critical analytical distinction with direct policy implications. Raw counts answer 'where do most deaths happen?' Rates answer 'where is risk highest?' Only the second supports policy.

Demographic Pattern

Within every state, adults aged 75 and over account for the largest share of influenza-related deaths. This is consistent with national CDC reporting and with the underlying biology of influenza severity in elderly populations.

Key Insight — Age structure shapes state-level risk

States with older population profiles consistently show higher mortality rates. Age-adjusted comparisons would refine the analysis further — without age adjustment, some of the apparent state-level variation is demographic, not behavioural.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — National mortality rate has been stable.

Statistical testing confirms no significant change in U.S. influenza mortality rate from 2009 to 2017. Rate stayed near 0.012%.

Finding 2 — Population strongly correlates with raw death counts (r ≈ 0.96).

Population alone explains most of the cross-state variation in raw counts — making raw counts useless as a comparative risk metric.

Finding 3 — Mortality rate, not raw counts, is the correct metric for state-by-state comparison.

Switching the metric reorders the map of 'most dangerous' states entirely — a critical insight for public-health decision-making.

Finding 4 — Adults aged 75+ account for the largest share of deaths.

Vaccination campaigns and preventive healthcare prioritization should focus on this demographic for greatest impact.
