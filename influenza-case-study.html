Project Overview

The project brief was operational, not academic: prepare for influenza season — right staff, right place, right time. Using past trends by state and demographics, plan when and how many medical staff to deploy across U.S. states during flu season.

The analytical challenge is choosing the metric that supports that decision. Raw death counts overwhelmingly track population, so they answer the wrong question — "where do most people live" — instead of "where is per-capita risk highest." The case study walks through that choice, then quantifies the resulting reorder of priority states.

Business questions: (1) Which states have the highest influenza burden after adjusting for population? (2) Has national mortality changed significantly across 2009–2017? (3) Which age groups should vaccination and outreach programs prioritize?

Temporal Trend (2009–2017)

Influenza is a recurring problem of stable magnitude, not a worsening one. National deaths sat in a tight 42K–50K band across the nine years; the population-adjusted rate hovered between 13.8 and 15.8 per 100,000, averaging 14.8 per 100k (≈0.015%).

Key Insight — Stable rate, growing population

The total-deaths picture looks volatile (2015 ≈ 50K, 2010 ≈ 43K). Once we divide through by population, the rate stays inside a narrow 14–16 per 100k band. The headline year-to-year swings are largely demographic, not epidemiological.

Population Drives Raw Counts

Across all 458 state-year observations, state population and raw influenza deaths are tightly linked — Pearson r = 0.956. This is exactly what makes raw counts misleading for cross-state comparison: at this strength, "biggest state" predicts "most deaths" almost mechanically. The real question — "where is per-capita risk highest?" — needs a different denominator.

Choose the Metric, Change the Answer

The metric choice doesn't shave a few positions off the leaderboard — it reorders it entirely. Five of the top-10 states by raw count drop out of the top-10 by rate, and five lower-population states (Hawaii, Massachusetts, Missouri, Mississippi, Alabama) rise into it.

Key Insight — Staffing follows the rate, not the count

If you deploy by raw deaths, you send people to where most people already live. If you deploy by rate, you protect the populations actually carrying the highest per-capita risk — and that's a fundamentally different list of states.

Spatial Distribution — The Tableau Dashboard

The three-layer Tableau map renders both metrics simultaneously: blue state-fill encodes total deaths, circle size encodes population, and circle color (white → red) encodes mortality rate per 100k. A Year filter lets stakeholders step through 2009–2017.

Demographic Pattern

Influenza mortality is overwhelmingly an elderly-population problem. Adults aged 85 and older alone account for 51% of all deaths; the 75+ population together accounts for 78%; the 65+ population together accounts for 91%. Mortality below age 55 is negligible.

Key Insight — Vaccination targeting is straightforward

This isn't a story about distributing effort across age bands — it's a story about one band. Vaccination outreach, antiviral stockpiling, and pre-season staffing for elderly-care facilities should be the dominant operational priority. The age signal is much stronger than the geographic signal.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — National mortality rate has been essentially flat.

Across 2009–2017 the rate stayed in a 13.8–15.8 per 100k band (≈0.015%). Apparent year-over-year volume changes are mostly demographic, not epidemiological. No statistically significant temporal trend.

Finding 2 — Population correlates near-perfectly with raw deaths (r = 0.956).

That makes raw counts a near-useless cross-state metric. Any "most-affected states" answer built on raw counts is mostly just listing the largest states.

Finding 3 — Mortality rate reorders the priority map entirely.

Switching metrics moves Hawaii, Massachusetts, Missouri, Mississippi, and Alabama into the top 10, and pushes California, Texas, Florida, Illinois, and Michigan out. Only New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and North Carolina survive both lists. Staffing decisions should reference the rate-ranked list.

Finding 4 — Vaccination targeting is age-driven.

The 85+ population accounts for 51% of all influenza-related deaths; the 75+ population accounts for 78%; the 65+ population accounts for 91%. Outreach and antiviral stockpiling should weight elderly-care facilities accordingly.
